# The study of CCP ideology: a field review

*Draft, 2026-11-12. Written from the author's reading, then checked citation by citation: 59
works, 56 verified as cited, 3 corrected (years and one affiliation), 0 not found; the four
load-bearing characterizations were checked against the sources and two were revised. The aim is orientation: which conversations exist, what each
treats as evidence, and where this corpus can add something. Not exhaustive.*

## The organizing question

The field keeps returning to one question in different forms: **does ideology do work in the
Chinese party-state, or is it a vocabulary laid over interests?** Each generation answered it
with the evidence it could reach: Maoist texts in the 1960s, elite speech and propaganda
systems after 1978, and since about 2010 large text corpora, censorship data and survey
experiments. The Xi era reopened the question, because the volume and centrality of doctrine
rose sharply.

## 1. Ideology as organization (the classic layer, 1950s to 1980s)

- **Franz Schurmann, *Ideology and Organization in Communist China* (1966).** The founding
  distinction between "pure" ideology (Marxism-Leninism, a world view) and "practical" ideology
  (Mao Zedong Thought, rules for action). Ideology is what lets an organization act coherently;
  it is studied through what the organization does, not what it believes.
- **Benjamin Schwartz, *Chinese Communism and the Rise of Mao* (1951)**, and the "legend of
  Maoism" debate with Karl Wittfogel in *The China Quarterly* (1960): was Mao's line a
  heterodox sinification or orthodox Leninism?
- **Stuart Schram**, *The Political Thought of Mao Tse-tung* (1963) and *The Thought of Mao
  Tse-tung* (1989). The text-critical tradition: what Mao said, in which edition, and how later
  official editions rewrote it.
- **Maurice Meisner, *Marxism, Maoism and Utopianism* (1982)**; **Arif Dirlik, *Marxism in the
  Chinese Revolution* (2005)**; **Nick Knight, *Rethinking Mao* (2007).** The Marxist-theory
  reading: how Chinese communism adapted (or abandoned) Marx.

What it treats as evidence: canonical texts and editions. The lesson for us is the
**edition-awareness**: the same formulation changes meaning when the official text is revised,
which is the problem `doc_identity`'s edition logic already handles for statutes.

## 2. Ideology as language: formulations and their policing

- **Michael Schoenhals, *Doing Things with Words in Chinese Politics* (1992).** The most
  directly useful book for this project. Official formulations (提法) are speech acts; the
  centre standardizes, bans and ranks them, and a change of wording is a change of policy.
- **Ji Fengyuan, *Linguistic Engineering: Language and Politics in Mao's China* (2004)**;
  **Perry Link, *An Anatomy of Chinese: Rhythm, Metaphor, Politics* (2013).**
- **Qian Gang and David Bandurski at the China Media Project** (founded 2004 at the University
  of Hong Kong), and Qian's *Watchwords: Reading China Through Its Party Vocabulary*: years of
  frequency analysis of Party-congress reports and People's Daily formulations, and the CMP
  Dictionary. Closest existing practice to the concept tracker.
- **Christian Sorace, *Shaken Authority* (2017).** Discourse as a constraint on officials: the
  Party binds itself to its own words, and local actors use those words back at it.

What it treats as evidence: which wording, where, when. **This is our method's ancestry.** The
gap: this work mostly reads top-level texts and People's Daily. It rarely measures how a
formulation *travels* across levels and speaker types, which is what the corpus can do.

## 3. After Mao: decline, adaptation, or management?

- **David Shambaugh, *China's Communist Party: Atrophy and Adaptation* (2008).** Ideology
  adapts rather than dies; the Party studied the Soviet collapse and drew lessons.
- **Heike Holbig**, "Remaking the CCP's Ideology: Determinants, Progress, and Limits under Hu
  Jintao" (*Journal of Current Chinese Affairs*, 2009), and **Holbig and Bruce Gilley**,
  "Reclaiming Legitimacy in China" (*Politics & Policy*, 2010). Ideology as one of several
  legitimation resources alongside performance and nationalism.
- **Anne-Marie Brady, *Marketing Dictatorship* (2008)**, and the edited volume *China's Thought
  Management* (2012). The propaganda system as a modernized persuasion industry.
- **Frank Pieke, *The Good Communist* (2009).** Party schools as the place where ideology is
  produced and taught to cadres.
- **Kalpana Misra, *From Post-Maoism to Post-Marxism* (1998).**
- **Victor Shih, "'Nauseating' Displays of Loyalty" (*Journal of Politics*, 2008).** A
  political-economy reading: in a system with norms against personality cults, exaggerated
  public praise is a credible loyalty signal because it is costly to the flatterer. Shih tests it
  on whether provincial newspapers echoed a campaign launched by the officials' own patron
  (Jiang Zemin's Three Represents, 2000-04), so the signal is aimed at a patron, not the regime
  at large. (That content matters less than conforming is our gloss, not Shih's claim.)

The live disagreement here: is ideology **belief**, **legitimation**, or **signal**? Shih's
answer predicts uptake that is uneven and tracks patronage; Schoenhals's predicts that the
content is policy. Both are testable on relay patterns.

## 4. Nationalism as the working ideology

- **Suisheng Zhao, *A Nation-State by Construction* (2004)**; **Zheng Wang, *Never Forget
  National Humiliation* (2012)**; **William Callahan, *China: The Pessoptimist Nation*
  (2010)**; **Jessica Chen Weiss, *Powerful Patriots* (2014).**

The argument: after 1989, patriotic education replaced Marxism as the effective glue. Weiss adds
the strategic use of popular nationalism in foreign policy. This literature explains why the
commentary register (观察者网) carries the anti-Western arguments the official register only
gestures at.

## 5. The intellectual field around the Party

- **Timothy Cheek, *The Intellectual in Modern Chinese History* (2015)**; **Cheek, David Ownby
  and Joshua Fogel (eds.), *Voices from the Chinese Century: Public Intellectual Debate from Contemporary China* (2019)**; **David Ownby's *Reading
  the China Dream*** (online translations of establishment intellectuals: 强世功, 汪晖, 张维为,
  白彤东 and others).
- **Wang Hui, *China's New Order* (2003)** and *The End of the Revolution* (2009); **Gloria
  Davies, *Worrying about China* (2007)**; **Mark Leonard, *What Does China Think?* (2008).**
- The New Left / liberal split of the 1990s and 2000s, the reception of Carl Schmitt and Leo
  Strauss, and Jiang Shigong's (强世功) readings of the Xi era.

What it treats as evidence: intellectuals' published arguments. It shows that "official
ideology" sits inside a wider field of semi-official thinkers who extend, test and sometimes
overshoot it. That is exactly the 张维为 / 谢茂松 versus CASS pattern in our civilization dispute.

## 6. The Xi era: the return of doctrine

- **Steve Tsang and Olivia Cheung, *The Political Thought of Xi Jinping* (2024).** The most
  systematic reading of Xi Thought as a coherent doctrine.
- **Kevin Rudd, *On Xi Jinping* (2024).** "Marxist nationalism": a leftward shift in politics
  and economics and a nationalist shift in foreign policy.
- **Jude Blanchette, *China's New Red Guards* (2019)**: the neo-Maoist current and its uses.
- **Elizabeth Economy, *The Third Revolution* (2018)**; **Carl Minzner, *End of an Era*
  (2018)**; **Joseph Fewsmith, *Rethinking Chinese Politics* (2021).**

The consensus: ideology became more central, more codified and more enforced after 2012. The
open question: is this belief, discipline, or the institutional form of personal authority?

## 7. Ideology and world order

- **Chinese-side theorists:** 赵汀阳 *天下体系* (2005; English *All under Heaven*, 2021); 阎学通
  (Yan Xuetong), *Ancient Chinese Thought, Modern Chinese Power* (2011) and *Leadership and the
  Rise of Great Powers* (2019); 张维为 (Zhang Weiwei), *The China Wave* (2012).
- **Critics and readers:** **William Callahan**, "Chinese Visions of World Order: Post-hegemonic
  or a New Hegemony?" (*International Studies Review*, 2008); **Nadège Rolland**, *China's
  Vision for a New World Order* (NBR, 2020); **Liza Tobin**, "Xi's Vision for Transforming
  Global Governance" (*Texas National Security Review*, 2018); **Rush Doshi**, *The Long Game*
  (2021), which reads Party texts systematically to infer grand strategy; **Elizabeth Economy**,
  *The World According to China* (2022); **Jessica Chen Weiss and Jeremy Wallace**, "Domestic
  Politics, China's Rise, and the Future of the Liberal International Order" (*International
  Organization*, 2021), and **Weiss**, "A World Safe for Autocracy?" (*Foreign Affairs*,
  July/August 2019).

The main fault line: does the "community of common destiny" (命运共同体) language describe an
**order-replacing** ambition (Rolland, Doshi, Tobin) or a **regime-securing** one that wants a
world safe for the Party, not a remade world (Weiss)? Our plenum finding, where the world
appears as a threat to the Party and the shared-future language disappears from a
Party-building text, is a small data point for the second reading inside domestic documents.

## 8. Quantitative political economy of propaganda and belief

- **Gary King, Jennifer Pan and Margaret Roberts**, *American Political Science Review* 2013
  (censorship targets collective action, not criticism) and 2017 (the "50-cent" fabricated
  posts as distraction); **Roberts, *Censored* (2018).**
- **Haifeng Huang**, "Propaganda as Signaling" (*Comparative Politics*, 2015): propaganda often
  works by signalling the government's capacity for social control, deterring challengers. He
  presents this as a complement to indoctrination, not a replacement.
- **Daniel Mattingly and Elaine Yao**, "How Soft Propaganda Persuades" (*Comparative Political
  Studies*, 2022).
- **Davide Cantoni, Yuyu Chen, David Yang, Noam Yuchtman and Y. Jane Zhang**, "Curriculum and
  Ideology" (*Journal of Political Economy*, 2017): the 2000s textbook reform measurably shifted
  student views.
- **Yuyu Chen and David Yang**, "The Impact of Media Censorship" (*American Economic Review*,
  2019).
- **Jennifer Pan and Yiqing Xu**, "China's Ideological Spectrum" (*Journal of Politics*, 2018):
  preferences are weakly constrained but spread over three highly correlated dimensions
  (political, economic, social). Preference for authoritarian rule goes with nationalism, state
  intervention and traditional values; it is not simply a pro- versus anti-regime split.
- **Daniela Stockmann, *Media Commercialization and Authoritarian Rule in China* (2012)**;
  **Maria Repnikova, *Media Politics in China* (2017).**

What it treats as evidence: experiments, surveys, leaked or scraped data. It studies the
**audience** end. Almost none of it measures the **producer** end at scale, which is how
doctrine is relayed, argued and filtered across the bureaucracy.

## Where this corpus fits

1. **Formulation travel at scale (Schoenhals by count).** Which speaker types adopt, relay,
   argue or ignore a formulation, and how fast. The weekly tracker is this.
2. **The signal-versus-content test (Shih vs Schoenhals).** Shih's test, at scale: does a
   locality echo a formulation more when its leaders are the originator's clients? That needs
   an officials-to-locality link (`officials.db` career records). If content is policy, provinces
   should adopt what carries projects (finding 5 of the global-discourse map: they relay the
   doctrine and implement the Belt and Road) and ignore what does not.
3. **The argued register.** The semi-official commentary layer (finding 2, 3, 4 disputes) is
   where Ownby and Cheek look qualitatively; the corpus lets it be dated and compared with
   the official register sentence by sentence.
4. **Edition and kept/cut analysis.** Schram's text-critical method applied to every speech and
   its source text, as in the Sun Yat-sen and plenum comparisons.
